Gratitude journal for recovery: how to start, and prompts that help

When you’re in recovery, your head can get stuck on everything that’s gone wrong. A gratitude journal is a small daily habit that helps you notice what’s going right. It’s simple, it’s free, and you can start tonight.

Nine months of gratitudes

I’m Dwayne, the founder of Hopewick. When I was getting clean, one of the things that helped me most was writing gratitudes. I did them every day for nine months, and it changed the way I think. Some days it was big stuff. Some days it was “I had a hot shower” or “I didn’t use today.” Both counted.

It didn’t fix everything, and it didn’t replace my counsellors or my meetings. But it slowly shifted where my mind went first thing in the morning and last thing at night. These days I’m a husband and a dad, and I still come back to gratitude when life gets heavy.

Why gratitude journalling can help in recovery

  • It interrupts the shame spiral. Guilt and shame are common after addiction. Writing down three good things gives your brain somewhere else to land.
  • It builds a routine. Early recovery needs structure. A two-minute journal at the same time each day is an easy anchor.
  • It gives you evidence on hard days. When a craving tells you nothing’s changed, you can read back through weeks of proof that it has.
  • It gives you something to talk about. Your journal can be a starting point for your next counselling session, meeting share or yarn with your sponsor.

Gratitude isn’t about pretending things are fine. You can be grateful and still be angry, sad or struggling. Both can be true on the same page.

How to start a recovery journal

  1. Pick a time. Morning, before bed, or straight after a meeting. Same time each day makes it stick.
  2. Keep it short. Three gratitudes and one line about how you’re feeling is plenty.
  3. Be specific. “My sister answered when I called” beats “my family.”
  4. Don’t chase a streak. Miss a day? Just write tonight. There’s nothing to restart.
  5. Read it back. Once a week, look over what you wrote. Notice the patterns.

Gratitude and sobriety journal prompts

  • What are three things, however small, that went okay today?
  • Who showed up for me this week, and how could I thank them?
  • What’s something I can do now that I couldn’t do when I was using?
  • What was hard today, and what got me through it?
  • If a craving came, what did I do instead?
  • What am I feeling right now? Angry, lonely, tired, scared, hopeful?
  • What’s one thing about my body or health I’m thankful for?
  • What’s one small thing I’m looking forward to tomorrow?

If a prompt brings up something heavy, that’s okay. Write it down and take it to your counsellor or AOD worker. If it becomes too much, call Lifeline on 13 11 14.

Frequently asked questions

Does a gratitude journal help with addiction recovery?

Many people in recovery find a daily gratitude practice helps them shift focus away from shame and build a steady routine. It isn’t a treatment on its own. It works best alongside counselling, meetings and support from people in your life.

What should I write in a recovery journal?

Start small: three things you’re grateful for, how you’re feeling in one word, and anything hard that came up. Prompts like “What got me through today?” or “If a craving came, what did I do instead?” are a good place to begin.

How long should I keep a gratitude journal?

There’s no set time. Founder Dwayne Stevens wrote gratitudes every day for nine months. Even two minutes a day can help. If you miss a day, just start again tonight.
